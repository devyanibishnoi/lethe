A database-native deletion layer for RAG knowledge bases

Making "your data has been deleted" a claim you can actually check, not one you have to take on faith.

This walks the whole system in the order data actually moves through it: the schema, the delete path, the proof that the delete really happened, then the live demo that puts all of it in front of you. Every section covers not just what the code does, but every decision that got made, what didn't work the first time, and what replaced it, including the places where an early assumption turned out to be wrong and got corrected in the open rather than quietly edited away.

The one-breath pitch

"Most vector databases soft-delete, the embedding is still physically recoverable on disk after you 'delete' it. Lethe partitions the index by tenant so a deletion only ever touches the affected shard, actually erases the data with a verified rewrite, and produces a cryptographically signed, hash-chained audit record, then we proved all of that at the byte level instead of just asserting it."

What problem this is actually solving

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems ground an LLM's answers in a private collection of documents, stored as vector embeddings so the system can find whichever documents are semantically relevant to a question. When someone asks for their data to be deleted, under GDPR's "right to erasure" or just as a matter of trust, most of these systems do something much weaker than it sounds: they mark the record as deleted in a way the application won't show you again, while the actual embedding, the numbers that encode what that document meant, is often still sitting untouched on disk. A soft delete isn't a delete, it's a promise that the application layer will politely look away.

Lethe is an attempt to close that gap at the database layer itself, not just at the application layer: partition the data so a deletion is cheap and scoped, actually rewrite the physical storage so the bytes are gone, sign and chain every deletion into a tamper-evident log so the claim "this was deleted" is independently checkable, and then, the part most such systems skip entirely, go and check it. Open the actual files Postgres writes to disk and look for the bytes directly, the same technique someone trying to recover "deleted" data would use, turned around and used as a test instead.

Worth being precise about what's actually new here, since it isn't either individual piece. Partitioned vector indexes aren't new. Hash-chained, tamper-evident logs aren't new. Prior work (PROBLEM_STATEMENT.md cites Chakraborttii et al., 2026) already demonstrated that deleted embeddings remain physically reconstructible from storage and proposed a basic signed proof-of-deletion. What that work stopped short of, and what this project exists to do, is combine partition-aware hard-delete with a tamper-evident log and then actually verify, at the byte level, that the fix closes the gap, including catching and correcting its own test's false confidence along the way rather than reporting the first clean-looking number. The measurement and the self-correction are the contribution, not either architectural piece in isolation.

How to read this document

It's organized the way the system is actually built, four layers, each with its own job: the data model and how it's physically laid out, the logic that performs and proves a deletion, the evaluation work that measures and verifies all of it, and the live system that makes all three tangible. Reading top to bottom tells the whole story in order; the sidebar is there for jumping straight to whichever layer you want.

01 Foundations

The schema, and why it's shaped this way

Five tables. The one that matters most is documents, because it's the only one that's partitioned, and partitioning is the entire architectural bet this project makes: a deletion should only ever cost you the size of the affected tenant's data, never the size of the whole system.

First, what a vector embedding actually is, before any of the schema makes sense

A vector embedding is a list of numbers, 384 of them here, that represents the meaning of a piece of text, produced by running it through a trained language model. Two sentences that mean similar things end up as two lists of numbers that are geometrically close together, two sentences about unrelated topics end up far apart. That closeness is what makes semantic search possible: "find documents about this topic" becomes "find the stored vectors nearest to this query's vector," a geometry problem instead of a keyword-matching problem.

"Close together" here specifically means cosine distance (the schema's indexes are built vector_cosine_ops): imagine every embedding as an arrow pointing from the origin out into 384-dimensional space. Cosine distance measures the angle between two arrows, not how long they are, two vectors pointing in nearly the same direction are "close" regardless of magnitude. That's deliberate for text: it's the direction (the meaning) that should matter, not an accident of how long the embedding happens to be.

Why you need an index at all, not just a loop

The honest way to find the true nearest neighbors is to compare a query against every single stored vector and sort by distance, an "exact" or "brute-force" search. That's correct, but it gets slow as the corpus grows, every query has to touch every row. Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) indexes, HNSW and ivfflat both are this, trade a small, usually negligible amount of accuracy for not having to check everything, by pre-organizing the vectors at insert time into a structure that lets a query skip most of the corpus. "Approximate" is the key word, and it's exactly why Layer 3 has a whole experiment dedicated to measuring how approximate, rather than assuming "close enough" by default.

The five tables, what each one is actually for

  • data_subjects — a real person or organization whose data exists in the system. Has a tenant_id, because the same system serves multiple tenants and a subject belongs to exactly one.
  • consent_status — one row per subject, whether they've consented. One-to-one with the subject, cascades on delete.
  • deletion_requests — "subject X wants to be forgotten." Starts pending, flips to completed once every document is actually gone. This is the thing a user submits; it's not the same thing as a single document deletion.
  • deletion_audit_log — the proof. One row per document actually erased, cryptographically signed, hash-chained to the row before it. Covered in full depth in Layer 2 below.
  • documents — the actual content and its embedding. This is the one that's partitioned.

Why partition by tenant specifically

documents is declared PARTITION BY LIST (tenant_id), with documents_tenant_1 and documents_tenant_2 as real, separate physical tables underneath it, each with its own HNSW index and its own ivfflat index. Not a view, not a filter, an actual separate table on disk per tenant.

That single design choice is what makes the whole "deletion doesn't cost the whole system" claim true. When hard_delete() rebuilds a tenant's index, it's physically rebuilding that tenant's table, there is no code path by which it could touch a different tenant's data, because the data isn't in the same file.

This got measured, not just asserted

The benchmarks in Layer 3 held one tenant fixed at 500 documents and varied how many other tenants (2, 4, 8, 16) and how much other data existed around it. The fixed tenant's own rebuild cost stayed flat, ~52–71ms, regardless of how many other tenants existed. A single shared index covering everyone's data at the same total size cost 223ms at 2 tenants, climbing to 932ms at 16, a gap that widens as the system grows, exactly the partitioning payoff this schema is betting on. See the Layer 3 section for the full table.

The composite primary key, and why it has to be that way

src/db/schema.sql 
PRIMARY KEY (id, tenant_id),
FOREIGN KEY (subject_id) REFERENCES data_subjects (id) ON DELETE CASCADE

Postgres requires the partition key to be part of every unique constraint on a partitioned table. id alone can't be the primary key here, because Postgres can't guarantee uniqueness of just id across partitions it enforces independently per-partition. (id, tenant_id) satisfies the constraint, and it's harmless in practice since every real lookup already knows which tenant it's working in.

Two index types on the exact same column, why both

src/db/schema.sql 
-- HNSW: cosine similarity
CREATE INDEX ... ON documents_tenant_1 USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops);

-- IVFFlat: cosine similarity
CREATE INDEX ... ON documents_tenant_1 USING ivfflat (embedding vector_cosine_ops)
WITH (lists = 10);

HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) builds a multi-layer graph at insert time: a sparse top layer with few, well-spread-out connections for jumping long distances quickly, and progressively denser layers below for fine-grained navigation, not unlike a highway system feeding down into local streets. A query starts at the sparse top layer, jumps toward the right neighborhood fast, then descends layer by layer, refining its position, without ever touching most of the graph. That structure is what gives HNSW strong recall and fast queries once built, but every insertion has to find its correct place in every relevant layer, so building and rebuilding the graph gets more expensive as it grows, not just proportionally (measured directly: going from 1,000 to 5,000 documents, 5× the data, made HNSW rebuild cost roughly 8× more expensive, not 5×, since each new node has more existing structure to navigate and connect into).

ivfflat (Inverted File with Flat compression) takes a completely different approach: at build time, it runs a clustering step (similar to k-means) that groups all the vectors into lists buckets (10, here) based on which are near each other. A query first figures out which bucket(s) its own vector would fall into, then only compares against the vectors actually stored in those buckets, never the whole table. Cheaper to build than a graph, but its accuracy is directly, explicitly tunable at query time via a probes setting, how many of the nearest buckets to actually search, rather than just the single closest one. More probes means checking more buckets, which costs more time but catches neighbors that happened to land just across a bucket boundary. Layer 3's stress test deliberately sets probes = 1 specifically to see what happens when that knob is turned all the way down.

Having both on the same column means the query planner picks whichever it estimates cheaper for a given query, confirmed directly via EXPLAIN during the recall testing in Layer 3, it genuinely does pick differently depending on data size and shape. The tradeoff this creates: there's no way to force a search to use one specific index type without disabling the other, which came up directly when testing recall, see Layer 3.

Why vector(384)

384 is not an arbitrary choice, it's the output dimensionality of all-MiniLM-L6-v2, the sentence-embedding model the corpus generator uses (src/eval/generate_corpus.py). The column width has to match whatever model actually produces the vectors, pick a different model, the schema has to change with it.

Why LIST partitioning specifically, not RANGE or HASH

Postgres gives you three partitioning strategies. RANGE is for continuous values with a natural ordering, dates, sizes, where a row belongs in a partition based on falling between two bounds, think "January's rows, February's rows." HASH is for spreading data evenly across N partitions when there's no meaningful grouping to preserve, you just want load balanced. LIST is for a small, known, discrete set of values where each partition corresponds to one specific value or a specific group of values, exactly what a tenant ID is: not a range, not something to hash away, a real, enumerable identity you want to keep physically separate and nameable. That's why documents_tenant_1 and documents_tenant_2 exist as literal, individually-named tables tied to one specific tenant UUID each (FOR VALUES IN ('00000000-...-001')), not an opaque hash bucket you'd have to look up to even know which tenant lives where.

Partition pruning, and a deliberate shortcut around it

Querying the parent documents table with a WHERE tenant_id = ... clause, Postgres's planner can prove at parse time that only one partition could possibly match, and skips scanning the others entirely, that's "partition pruning," the mechanism that makes partitioning actually pay off instead of just being organizational.

hard_delete() doesn't even rely on pruning, it goes a step further: TENANT_CONFIG (in src/logic/hard_delete.py) maps each tenant UUID directly to its child table's literal name, documents_tenant_1, and every statement, the DELETE, the VACUUM FULL, targets that child table by name directly, never the parent. No planner decision involved at all, just addressing the one physical table that could possibly be affected. That's the most literal form the partitioning guarantee can take: the code doesn't trust the planner to prune correctly, it makes it structurally impossible to touch the wrong tenant's table.

The lists = 10 parameter on ivfflat

ivfflat's accuracy and speed both come from how it buckets vectors into lists clusters (a k-means-like clustering step at index-build time); a commonly cited heuristic is roughly the square root of the expected row count. At the corpus sizes this project actually tests against (hundreds to a few thousand documents per tenant), 10 lists is a reasonable fit; it's a parameter that would need to grow if the corpus grew substantially, it's not self-tuning.

Why applying schema.sql on an empty database logs a "low recall" notice

CREATE INDEX ... USING ivfflat on a table with zero or very few rows warns that recall will be poor, because the clustering step has nothing meaningful to cluster yet, a handful of points can't form 10 sensible buckets. Expected, not an error, resolves itself once real data is loaded and the index gets rebuilt against it.

An open question this document can't answer on its own

documents has a deleted BOOLEAN NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE column. It gets written on insert (src/db/db.py), but nothing in the active delete path ever reads or sets it to TRUE, hard_delete() performs a real DELETE, not a flag flip. Most likely an earlier soft-delete design that got superseded once the hard-delete path was built, left in place rather than removed, but that's an inference, not a confirmed answer, worth tracking down against the schema's own history rather than guessing.

02 Core logic

Hard delete, signing, and the chain of proof

This is the file that changed three separate times over the course of this investigation, src/logic/hard_delete.py, and every change was caused by actually testing a claim the earlier version was making and finding it false. Walking through the final code first, then the full history of why it looks like this now.

Signing a deletion, step by step

src/logic/sign_deletion.py 
def sign_deletion(content, document_id):
    content_hash = hash_content(content)          # SHA-256 of the document's own content
    previous_hash = get_previous_hash()            # the last entry's hash, or a genesis hash
    signed_at = get_current_timestamp()

    message = build_message(document_id, content_hash, signed_at, previous_hash)
    signature = sign_message(message, private_key)  # ECDSA over SHA-256

    # self-check before this ever reaches the database (see note below)
    if not verify_signature(message, signature, public_key):
        raise RuntimeError(...)

    return {content_hash, signature, signed_at, previous_hash}

The message that actually gets signed is f"{document_id}, {content_hash}, {signed_at}, {previous_hash}", encoded to bytes. Every one of those four fields has to be reproduced byte-for-byte identical anywhere this gets re-verified later, ECDSA verifies an exact byte string, not a semantically-equivalent one. That exactness requirement is not theoretical, it caused a real bug, covered below.

The hash chain

previous_hash is the previous audit log row's deleted_hash (fetched via ORDER BY signed_at DESC LIMIT 1). The very first entry ever chains to a genesis value, 69 zero characters. Every subsequent entry points backward, so the whole table forms one single chain across every tenant and every subject, not a separate chain per subject. Corrupting or deleting any one row breaks the link for whatever comes after it.

Verifying it

src/logic/verify_deletion.py 
def verify_chain():
    # every row, oldest to newest
    for row in rows:
        if previous_hash != expected_previous_hash:
            return False          # link broken, stop immediately
        if not verify_deletion(...):
            return False          # signature invalid, stop immediately
        expected_previous_hash = content_hash
    return True
Know this limitation before you're asked about it

verify_chain() returns a single boolean. It short-circuits on the very first break it finds, which means it can tell you something is wrong, but not where. Confirmed directly in testing: once one row anywhere in the whole table is bad, the function returns False forever after, with no way to distinguish "the same old problem" from "something new just broke." A real incident-response version would need to report the first bad entry's position, not just a boolean. This is flagged as a known gap, not hidden.

The final hard_delete(), and what it actually does now

src/logic/hard_delete.py 
document = get_document(document_id, tenant_id)
signing_data = sign_deletion(document[3], document_id)
save_audit_log(conn, deletion_request_id, document_id, signing_data)

cur.execute(f"DELETE FROM {partition} WHERE id = %s AND tenant_id = %s", ...)
conn.commit()

conn.autocommit = True
cur.execute(f"VACUUM FULL {partition}")   # one statement, closes the loop

Sign, log, delete, commit, then one VACUUM FULL on the whole partition. That single statement rewrites the partition's heap file and rebuilds every index on it, from scratch, as part of the rewrite. It's the final form of three iterations, each one driven by a claim that got tested and found wrong.

Decision 1 · the ivfflat index was never touched

Gap found

The very first version only rebuilt the HNSW index after a delete.

A self-review of the paper draft flagged that the "deletion is real" claim was weaker than the write-up implied: hard_delete() rebuilt HNSW but never touched ivfflat on the exact same column, and never ran VACUUM at all. ivfflat stores its own copy of each vector inside its own index pages, not just a pointer into the heap. Leaving it un-rebuilt meant a deleted embedding's bytes stayed physically present in the ivfflat file indefinitely. This directly matched the research gap PROBLEM_STATEMENT.md is built around, soft-delete persistence despite a reported deletion, so it was a real implementation gap, not just a wording issue.

Fixed by adding ivfflat_index to TENANT_CONFIG and running REINDEX INDEX on both. Verified for real, not just read through: built a one-off document, called the fixed function, confirmed the row was gone from a fresh SELECT and both indexes reported indisvalid = true after their rebuild.

A correction caught before it shipped, worth knowing the reasoning behind

The first instinct was that the un-rebuilt ivfflat index meant a deleted document could still show up in live search results. That instinct is wrong, and it's worth understanding why: Postgres checks heap tuple visibility (MVCC) on every single index scan, regardless of whether the index itself has been rebuilt. A stale index entry just becomes a dangling pointer that gets filtered out at scan time, it can never resurrect a deleted row into a query result. The real exposure is forensic, not functional: the embedding's bytes stay physically present in the index pages and the heap, recoverable by someone with raw page or disk access, even though no ordinary SQL query would ever surface it. Getting this distinction right mattered, the stronger (and false) "deleted docs could leak into search" version would have been a worse claim to put in a paper than the true, narrower one.

First, what VACUUM actually is, cold, before the decisions that depend on it

The root problem it exists to solve: when Postgres deletes a row, it doesn't erase the bytes. It just flags that row as dead (flips a hidden field called xmax) and leaves it exactly where it was. This is MVCC (multi-version concurrency control): Postgres lets many transactions read the database at once without blocking each other, by keeping old row versions around until it's certain no transaction still in flight could need them. Erase rows the instant they're deleted, and a transaction that started a moment earlier, whose snapshot of the world still includes that row, would suddenly see broken data.

What VACUUM actually does: it's the cleanup crew. It scans the table, finds rows that are dead and that no live transaction could possibly still need, and marks that space "free, safe to write something new here." Picture a whiteboard divided into fixed-size boxes: VACUUM crosses a box off a "taken" list so a new line can go there later, but it doesn't scrub the box clean, the old marks are still physically visible until something new actually gets written on top of them.

This is the exact gap the whole project is built around

"Space marked reusable" is not the same thing as "bytes overwritten." Open the raw file on disk right after a plain VACUUM, and the deleted embedding's numbers are still sitting there in plain sight, which is exactly what the 20-trial byte search in test_storage_erasure.py proved directly, not just reasoned about.

Why it can't run inside a transaction: VACUUM has to ask "is there any transaction anywhere in the database that could still need this row?", a question about the whole system's state, not something that makes sense from inside one transaction's own private snapshot. Postgres just refuses to let it run inside one. That's the entire reason the code needs conn.autocommit = True after the delete has already committed, shown in the final code above.

Plain VACUUM vs. VACUUM FULL, the tradeoff the whole decision history pivots on

  • Plain VACUUM reuses space in place. Cheap, doesn't lock anyone out of reading. But it never overwrites, so the old bytes can sit there indefinitely, until some future insert happens to land in that exact spot.
  • VACUUM FULL does a complete rewrite, builds a brand-new file containing only the live rows, throws the old file away entirely. A real, physical guarantee the old bytes are gone, the same mechanism REINDEX uses for an index. The cost: an exclusive lock on the whole table while it works, and roughly double the disk space while old and new briefly coexist.

That cheap-but-leaky vs. complete-but-locking tradeoff is the entire reason hard_delete() changed three times. It started on plain VACUUM (leaky), on the assumption that was the safer default because it supposedly didn't block reads, Decision 4 below is where a real concurrency test proved that assumption false, which removed the only reason left to avoid the stronger option.

Decision 2 · a plain DELETE doesn't erase bytes

Gap found

DELETE only marks the heap row dead under MVCC.

Postgres doesn't overwrite a deleted row's bytes immediately, it flips a flag (xmax) and leaves the bytes in place until VACUUM later marks that space reusable, and even then, reusable is not the same as overwritten. VACUUM cannot run inside a transaction block at all (a hard Postgres rule, it needs to update visibility-map bits outside of any transaction's own snapshot), which is why it has to be a separate, later, autocommit step, not something that can just be added to the same transaction as the delete.

The byte-level verification methodology itself, first applied here

Up to this point, every claim about deletion had been checked by asking Postgres questions about itself, row counts, pg_stat_user_tables, pg_index.indisvalid. None of that is the same kind of evidence as actually opening the file and looking. So the methodology changed: resolve the real on-disk file for the heap and each index via pg_relation_filepath(), run CHECKPOINT first (a raw file read can otherwise miss a page still sitting only in memory), pull the real file out of the Docker container with docker cp, and search its raw bytes in Python for the deleted embedding's exact pattern. This is, not coincidentally, the same angle the prior paper this project sits next to (Chakraborttii et al., cited in PROBLEM_STATEMENT.md) used to originally find the vulnerability, turned back around to check whether the fix actually closes it.

Re-ran the full benchmark suite after the ivfflat + VACUUM fix, since a fix nobody re-measures is just a hope. Numbers got honestly worse, the correct direction, since the fix makes hard_delete() do strictly more real work:

MetricBefore fixAfter fix
Single hard-delete latency~68 ms~97–141 ms
Cascading erasure, 50 docs~4150–4400 ms~5000–6290 ms
Index rebuild cost (HNSW only, 5000 docs)~309–333 ms~309–373 ms, unchanged

The HNSW-only rebuild number staying flat is a deliberate control: that benchmark only ever measures HNSW rebuild in isolation, so it being unaffected confirms the slowdown elsewhere really is from the new ivfflat+VACUUM work, not unrelated noise in the environment.

Decision 3 · the first REINDEX fix wasn't actually reliable

Overturned, 65% failure rate

REINDEX was running inside the same transaction as the DELETE, before it committed.

13 / 20

Expanding the storage test to run over 20 documents (not the one document the earlier spot-check used), against a real, by-then much larger tenant partition, gave a completely different result than the first check had: the deleted embedding's pattern was still found in HNSW and ivfflat in 13 of 20 trials, not 0.

The investigation into why, step by step, rather than just reporting whichever number looked better: first ruled out a docker cp truncation bug, the copied file's size matched the real file size inside the container exactly, byte for byte. Then ruled out a corpus-generator bug, could two different documents share an embedding by chance? The random generation space (names × IPs × hostnames × second-precision timestamps over 90 days) is astronomically too large for that. Then ran controlled single-document checks against the current, larger database, and found the real answer: even a freshly inserted, still-alive document's own embedding was sometimes not findable by this same search in its own HNSW index, on some trials and not others, nothing about the test itself changed between runs.

That meant the search methodology itself was the unreliable part at scale, not evidence about REINDEX one way or the other, almost certainly because HNSW's graph-node page-packing doesn't always lay a vector's floats down as one contiguous run the way a flat heap row does. The small early tests had looked clean purely because the index was still tiny and simply laid out. So a different test was needed: not "is the pattern findable", but "does when REINDEX runs relative to the commit matter". Decision 4 · "this design doesn't block reads" was never tested

Overturned, assumption never checked

Assumed the three-step design (unlike a heavier alternative, VACUUM FULL) didn't block concurrent reads.

5 / 5 trials, both designs

This claim had been stated as settled fact across multiple earlier write-ups, carried over from general Postgres knowledge (REINDEX is "lighter" than VACUUM FULL), never once tested against this specific code. Checked it the same careful way as the REINDEX investigation: first the smallest possible isolated diagnostic, a bare SELECT count(*) running in a background thread against a bare REINDEX call with nothing else happening, to rule out the result being an artifact of the test harness rather than the database. One query overlapped the window, started 12ms in, and didn't complete until the instant the lock released, with a latency matching the lock duration almost exactly, the textbook signature of being blocked, not merely slow. Plain REINDEX INDEX (not CONCURRENTLY) does take a lock strong enough to stall an ordinary read, documented Postgres behavior that had simply never been checked here before being asserted.

Built the full version after that: a background thread looping real similarity_search() calls, the exact function the live /search endpoint uses, while a deletion ran on the main thread, 5 repeats per design, the measurement window narrowed to just the lock-holding statements, separated from the non-blocking setup work (signing, the audit write, the bare DELETE) around them:

Current design (REINDEX ×2 + VACUUM)VACUUM FULL
Trials showing a blocked query5/55/5
Mean lock-window duration390.3 ms402.0 ms
Worst blocked-query latency376.0 ms432.5 ms

A concurrent search issued during the lock window doesn't error, it queues and waits, then completes right as the lock releases, exactly the same signature VACUUM FULL produces. Both designs block, for a similar duration. That removed the one argument that had been keeping the three-step design as the assumed default.

The one honest caveat that's still open, say this if asked "is this production-ready"

VACUUM FULL needs roughly double the disk space temporarily, it writes a full second copy of the table before dropping the old one. Not stress-tested at a scale where that would actually matter here, but a real operational consideration for running this at production volume, separate from the latency and locking costs that were measured directly.

Two more things worth knowing cold

A one-character bug that broke a real signature

The deletion certificate has to re-emit the exact signed_at string that was originally signed. Python's default datetime formatting uses a space; the default JSON encoding used by the API uses T. One character, and it was enough to make an otherwise-valid signature fail re-verification. Fixed in deletion_certificate.py by explicitly calling str(signed_at) instead of letting it serialize however JSON wants to.

One signature failed self-verification once, and never again

During a 71-signature benchmark run, one signature failed its own self-check, with no reproducible cause after ruling out stale keys, timestamp collisions, duplicate documents. Never recurred across 142 further signatures. Rather than dismiss it, the self-verification step in sign_deletion() (shown above) exists specifically so a future recurrence fails loudly at the moment of signing, not discovered cold during an audit days later.

03 Evaluation

Proving it, instead of claiming it

Every number on the site traces back to a script in src/eval/, logged to docs/benchmark_results.csv. The methodology that actually matters most here isn't the latency numbers, it's the byte-level verification, searching the literal bytes Postgres writes to disk, the same technique that exposes the soft-delete problem in the first place, turned back around to check whether the fix actually works.

Getting started, and the first real lesson

Built src/eval/generate_corpus.py, the four-template synthetic incident-log generator, embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, verified with a round-trip insert/fetch test before trusting it for anything else.

Then built src/eval/run_benchmarks.py, logging to a long/tidy CSV (one row per measurement, a metric label column, so a new measurement type never needs a schema change). The first full run produced misleading numbers: a crashed earlier run had already grown the tenant partition to 5,000 documents, so the "corpus size" labels on the rebuild-cost sweep (100/500/1000) didn't actually reflect the true index size at measurement time, the script was silently measuring a 5,000-row index while labeling the result "100". Fixed by adding an explicit reset step at the start of every run rather than assuming a clean database, a small lesson that mattered a lot later: never trust a benchmark script's own labels without checking what state it actually started from.

What "recall@k" actually means, since it's used constantly from here on

If the true k nearest neighbors to a query are some specific set of documents, and an approximate search's own top-k result shares N of those same documents, recall@k is N / k. A score of 1.0 means the approximate index found exactly the same top-k a perfect, exhaustive search would have, nothing missed. The specific number above, 0.995, is a narrower version of that idea: not recall against true exhaustive search (that version comes later), but how much the top-10 for the same 20 queries changed after deleting 20 unrelated documents elsewhere in the corpus, a check that deleting someone's data doesn't quietly degrade search quality for everyone else.

This is also where the one-character timestamp bug and the unexplained-signature anomaly, both covered in Layer 2 above, actually got found, building the certificate page is what exposed the timestamp-formatting mismatch, and a 71-signature benchmark run is what surfaced the anomaly.

Why so much of this is "run it 20 times" or "3 repeats," not just once

A single measurement can be influenced by things that have nothing to do with what's actually being tested, which process the OS scheduled a moment earlier, whether a page happened to already be cached, ordinary background noise on a shared, non-isolated development laptop. One number can't tell you whether an effect is real or just that kind of noise. Repeating a measurement and reporting the range, not just a mean, is how you tell the two apart, and it's the single most repeated methodological choice across this whole investigation: 20 trials for every byte-level search, 3 repeats for every scaling and timing comparison, 5 repeats for the concurrency test, 20 repeats for VACUUM FULL timing. More than once, a single-run result that looked clean turned out to be an artifact once repeated (the index-residue bug below is the clearest example, a one-document test showed 0%, a 20-trial test on real data showed 65%, same code, the only thing that changed was sample size).

How the byte-level search actually works

A quick grounding, if Docker and raw page files aren't familiar territory

Postgres here runs inside a Docker container, essentially an isolated mini-computer with its own private filesystem, separate from the host machine's. Every table and index is ultimately just a file inside that container's filesystem; pg_relation_filepath() is a Postgres function that answers "where, exactly, on your own internal disk, does this specific table or index actually live," and docker cp is the command that reaches into the container and copies that file back out to the host machine, where Python can open it directly, no SQL, no Postgres API involved at all once the file is out, just raw bytes.

  1. Fetch a document's real stored embedding back from Postgres after insert (not the Python list that was sent in, the one actually stored).
  2. Pack it the way pgvector stores it on disk: a flat sequence of IEEE-754 float4s, little-endian, via struct.pack(f"<384f", *embedding), a distinctive 1,536-byte pattern.
  3. Resolve the real on-disk file path for the heap partition and each index with pg_relation_filepath().
  4. Run CHECKPOINT to force anything sitting only in memory out to the actual file. Postgres doesn't write every change to disk the instant it happens, for performance it buffers pages in memory (shared_buffers) and flushes them later on its own schedule; CHECKPOINT forces that flush immediately, otherwise a raw file read could miss a change that's real but still only sitting in memory.
  5. Pull the real file out of the Docker container with docker cp, and search its raw bytes for the pattern in Python.
A real methodology lesson worth mentioning if asked

This search is reliable for the heap file (100% consistent at every scale tested). It is not reliable for HNSW/ivfflat index files once the index has real size, a freshly-inserted, still-alive document's own embedding was sometimes not findable in its own index by this same method, almost certainly because HNSW's graph-node packing doesn't always lay a vector down as one contiguous run the way a heap row does. Caught by running the test over 20 trials instead of one and noticing the result wasn't stable, small early tests looked clean purely because the index was still tiny.

Two more things the byte-level search turned up

The embedding is also sitting in the write-ahead log

Same docker cp-and-search technique, pointed at the live pg_wal segment files instead of the heap or index files. Found: yes, the deleted embedding's full pattern is findable there too. WAL is append-only and isn't rewritten by REINDEX or VACUUM, so this is a real, separate forensic exposure surface beyond the heap and index story, worth stating plainly as a further limitation rather than leaving unexamined.

The soft-delete baseline, and what the extra work actually buys

A plain DELETE, no reindex, no vacuum, the same byte-search methodology run against it: the pattern survives in the heap and both indexes afterward, every time. That's the direct, measured answer to "what does all this extra work buy you", two index rebuilds and a vacuum (or, now, one VACUUM FULL) in exchange for erasure that's actually verified, instead of none at all.

Proving verify_chain() actually catches a deleted (not just mutated) row

audit.html's existing "corrupt-for-demo" button mutates one stored hash in place, a different failure mode from deleting a whole row outright. Built src/eval/test_chain_gap_detection.py to test the second case. First run surfaced something unexpected on its own: verify_chain() was already returning False on the real audit history before the test touched anything, because of a stale corrupt-for-demo fixture left from earlier live browser testing. verify_chain() short-circuits on the first break it finds, so once any row anywhere is broken, the whole-table boolean is False forever after, there was no way to tell from that boolean alone whether a new break had just happened. Worked around it by building a disposable 4-entry segment, verifying its links directly rather than through the global function, then deleting the middle entry outright: the entry that used to follow it now has a previous_hash matching no existing row, exactly the failure mode verify_chain() is built to catch. Confirmed.

Does the ANN index actually approximate well?

What EXPLAIN is, since it's the tool this entire section leans on

Postgres doesn't run a query the way it's written, it first builds a plan: a tree of concrete steps like "scan this index," "sort these rows," "sequentially scan this whole table," then estimates the cost of each candidate plan and picks the cheapest one. EXPLAIN <query> prints that chosen plan instead of running the query for its results, so it's the only direct way to see whether the planner actually used the ivfflat or HNSW index for a given vector search, or quietly fell back to scanning every row (a Seq Scan), which would make a "the index is approximating well" claim meaningless, since there would have been no approximation happening at all. Settings like enable_indexscan/enable_bitmapscan only ask the planner to avoid those plan types, they don't force it, which is exactly why the work below double-checked with EXPLAIN rather than trusting the setting had taken effect.

The existing recall_at_k metric only measured overlap of the ANN top-k before vs. after an unrelated deletion, not recall against real ground truth. Built measure_recall_vs_exact(): for 20 random queries, the true top-10 via a forced sequential scan (SET enable_indexscan = off; SET enable_bitmapscan = off, confirmed via EXPLAIN that this actually produces a Seq Scan, not just trusting the setting took effect), compared against the planner's normal ANN path. Result at corpus size 4,980: 1.0 recall, ivfflat (whichever the planner picked that run, also logged via EXPLAIN since pgvector gives no way to pin one index type over the other) returned exactly the same top-10 as exhaustive search for every query. Resisted the urge to oversell this: a small, synthetic, template-generated corpus scoring perfectly isn't proof the index is exact in general, it's evidence this corpus's embeddings are well-separated enough that approximation had no real work to do at this scale.

That raised a real question worth actually testing rather than just caveating: why is recall coming out perfect? The corpus (src/eval/generate_corpus.py) is four sentence templates with randomized names/IPs/timestamps filled in. Pairwise similarity across 200 documents: mean 0.431, 4.5% of all pairs exceed 0.9 similarity, max pairwise similarity 0.999, near-duplicates from the same template with just a name or IP swapped. Reran this independently later (0.435 vs. 0.431 the second time), close enough to trust it's a real property of the corpus, not a fluke of one random sample. That long tail of near-duplicate pairs is a direct, measured reason default-setting recall looked artificially perfect, when a query has a cluster of near-identical neighbors, an approximate index has very little room to get the top-10 wrong even approximately.

An explanation isn't a test, though. So a real stress test was added: two tables, each pinned to one index type (so the index under test is actually pinned, not planner-chosen the way recall_vs_exact_knn is), with accuracy deliberately starved (ivfflat.probes = 1 vs. a sensible default of searching more lists, hnsw.ef_search = 10 vs. a default of 40), checked against true exhaustive search.

The concurrency test that reversed a design decision

Covered from Layer 2's side above; the methodology detail worth knowing: a background thread looped real similarity_search() calls while a deletion ran on the main thread, 5 repeats per design, with the measurement window narrowed to just the lock-holding statements (REINDEX/VACUUM), separated from the non-blocking setup work (signing, the audit write, the bare DELETE) around them. Verified first with the smallest possible isolated check, a bare SELECT count(*) against a bare VACUUM FULL call, before trusting the fuller version.
